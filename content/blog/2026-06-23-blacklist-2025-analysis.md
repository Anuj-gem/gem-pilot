<p>The Black List is one of the most respected institutions in Hollywood. Every year it surfaces unproduced scripts that development executives genuinely admire — strong voices, bold ideas, real craft. A lot of great things have come out of it.</p>

<p>And yet most scripts on it never get made.</p>

<p>That is not a script quality problem. It is a perspective problem. The way most of the industry evaluates scripts — quickly, impressionistically, through a lens built for a market that no longer fully exists — misses a significant amount of what is actually there. And if it is happening on the most prestigious unproduced screenplay list in Hollywood, it is happening everywhere.</p>

<p>We have been thinking about this for a while. GEM is a development company — for those who don't know us, we have built creative networks with close to a billion views across social media, and we are actively developing a feature film and a series while looking for more projects to partner on. In that process we kept encountering Black List scripts. We were genuinely impressed by the quality of the work. So we decided to look at all of them.</p>

<p><strong>We analyzed every script on the 2025 Black List — all 74 — the same way we approach anything we might want to produce.</strong></p>

<div class="q-block">
  <p>Not from loglines. Not from coverage. We asked: What would this cost? Who is the audience? What is the actual path to attention? What moments would travel? Could it work theatrically? As a prestige play? Could it be made leaner? Is there a version of this that becomes obvious once you stop looking at it through the default Hollywood lens?</p>
</div>

<p>That number mostly describes what happens when you apply a single lens — theatrical, wide release — to seventy-four scripts that weren't all built for it.</p>

<p>Most weren't. That is the honest read. But it is also not the only question worth asking.</p>

<p>The distribution landscape has changed. A project with a distinct voice and a specific audience has more paths than it did ten years ago — streaming acquisitions, the awards circuit, international rights, prestige theatrical runs. Scripts that look non-commercial through one lens can look very different through another. The Black List keeps surfacing work that deserves to be made. What hasn't kept pace is the framework for thinking about how to make it.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">
  <p>The distribution landscape has changed. The evaluation lens hasn't.</p>
</div>

<p>When you look at the list through a wider lens, the picture gets more interesting.</p>

<p>A lot of the scripts are exactly as difficult to produce as you would expect: expensive, tonally narrow, hard to market through traditional channels. But inside the list are real opportunities — scripts with a genuine path once you stop asking whether they fit the old model and start asking how the modern era actually gets behind them.</p>
