<p>The Black List is one of the most respected institutions in Hollywood. Every year it surfaces unproduced scripts that development executives genuinely admire — strong voices, bold ideas, real craft. A lot of great things have come out of it.</p>

<p>And yet most scripts on it never get made.</p>

<p>We decided to look at all of them. GEM is a development company — we have built creative networks with close to a billion views across social media, and we are actively developing a feature film and a series. In that process we kept encountering Black List scripts. We were genuinely impressed. So we worked through every script on the 2025 Black List the same way we approach anything we might want to produce.</p>

<p>The first number that comes out:</p>

<p>When you read the slate as one number, the answer is pass. But a slate is not one thing. The aggregate math hides a distribution — and inside that distribution are real pockets of opportunity that the surface read consistently misses.</p>

<p>Not everything on the list has a path. Some of these scripts, despite being well-crafted, don't project to viability on any lens we apply. We genuinely hope we are wrong about those — we wish every writer on it tremendous success, and they deserve it. But there are projects on this list where we think people are clearly leaving money on the table. We found three distinct kinds.</p>

---

<div class="bucket">
  <span class="bucket-label">Bucket one</span>
  <div class="bucket-title">The commercial misread: scripts whose economics work, once you stop reading the logline</div>

<p>A subset of scripts on this list looks modest on paper. Not the prestige narratives. Not the ones generating industry buzz. But when you go past the logline — when you read for what actually happens on screen, how the film moves, what it asks an audience to feel — a completely different picture emerges.</p>

<p>What they share: a point of view so specific it cannot be ignored. Settings and sequences built for how content actually spreads now — moments that get clipped, screenshots that circulate, images that lodge. Premises aimed squarely at the audience that shows up opening weekend and brings friends. Horror is where this is clearest. A script can read as familiar territory in summary and be doing something genuinely original in execution. The surface pass will never tell you that.</p>

<p>The economics of this lane are real. At the right budget — $4M to $8M — the expected return approaches or clears theatrical break-even before streaming enters the picture. That is rare on this list. It is also being systematically overlooked because the logline does not announce it.</p>

<div class="bucket">
  <span class="bucket-label">Bucket two</span>
  <div class="bucket-title">The prestige + streaming floor play: where the theatrical math doesn't work, but the case builds when you stack the factors</div>

<p>A different subset. Scripts where a straight theatrical projection genuinely says no — and where the old Hollywood read would stop there. The new one doesn't.</p>

<p>These are scripts with the kind of emotional ambition that demands a performance. Stories with a cultural specificity and thematic weight that drives high-level casting conversations — the kind of project where a name actor decides this is the one they reach for, accepts below-market pay, and brings an audience that did not come from the marketing budget. Narratives with the structural qualities that critics can actually write about at length, that travel to international markets, that generate the kind of discourse that persists after opening weekend.</p>

<p>None of those factors alone greenlights a film. Combined, they build a thesis. A streaming pre-sale puts a floor under the downside. An awards trajectory — and some of these scripts have unmistakable patterns of it — multiplies downstream value in every window. The talent draw reduces effective cost. You are not betting on a box office hit. You are buying something with bounded downside and compounding upside.</p>

<p>The Black List has scripts with this profile. Scripts that carry the weight of things that get noticed — not because a model says so, but because you can see it in the craft, in the performance demands, in the kind of story it is. The theatrical projection says loser. That is where most reads end. It should not be.</p>
</div>

<div class="bucket">
  <span class="bucket-label">Bucket three</span>
  <div class="bucket-title">The asymmetric ceiling play: where the expected case is modest and the upside is enormous</div>

<p>A third group. Scripts where the expected case — the most likely outcome under normal conditions — is modest. And where something else is also true: the ceiling is real, and it is large.</p>

<div class="num-pair">
  <div class="np-item">
    <span class="np-num">$10M</span>
    <span class="np-label">Median expected box office across the slate</span>
  </div>
  <div class="np-item">
    <span class="np-num">$52M</span>
    <span class="np-label">Median breakout ceiling across the same slate</span>
  </div>
</div>

<p>That gap — between what a script is expected to make and what it could make under the right conditions — is the value the surface read buries. It does not show up in a coverage report. It shows up when you understand what creates ceiling: IP that travels internationally, narrative structures that support franchise expansion, premises that adapt across formats, genre dynamics that reward the rare film that gets it exactly right.</p>

<p>With streaming as the floor — which most serious productions now have access to — the downside on these is bounded. The upside is asymmetric. The industry systematically underprices this because it is optimizing for expected case. Expected case is not the only thing worth pricing.</p>

<p>The Black List has scripts with this kind of ceiling. Scripts where the expected case is modest enough to pass on and the upside — given the right conditions — is genuinely large. These require a different analytical frame than the one most development processes apply. Not harder. Just different.</p>

---

<p>The Black List is the best possible test case for this argument. These are scripts that have already been read — vetted, celebrated, widely circulated among people with real taste and real access. The quality has been vouched for. And still, most of them will not get made.</p>

<p>The gap is not quality. It is evaluation method. And knowledge base.</p>

<p>Modern distribution gives producers more levers than the framework most development processes are built around. Streaming floors that bound downside. Social behavior that changes what commercial means. International co-production that changes budget math. Awards economics that compound across every window long after theatrical. Reading a script in 2026 with a 2005 framework means you are systematically leaving real opportunities on the table — not because you lack taste, but because the infrastructure to find them at scale does not exist in most rooms.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">
  <p>The talent has never been more accessible. The willingness to go look for it is what has been missing.</p>
</div>

<p>We have reviewed thousands of scripts with this methodology — the Black List was one slice. What we keep finding is that the miss rate is high, and it is not random. It is the predictable result of fast reads, conventional lenses, and processes that were not built for the distribution landscape that now exists.</p>

<p>We have projects in active development that we found exactly this way. Scripts that would not survive a surface read. Writers who have never had a room hear their work. When you go deep — when you understand what the material structurally resembles, what creates the ceiling, what makes content travel — the case for the right projects is clear.</p>

<p>The question we get most often is how — how we are seeing what we are seeing. That is a longer conversation. But the short answer is that we have built the infrastructure to go deep at scale: a substantive body of research on what drives commercial performance across every distribution window, how genre and budget interact, what makes content travel, what patterns precede the outcomes people are still trying to predict from gut alone. The creative judgment still matters. It always will. But that judgment gets sharper when it has something real to draw from.</p>

<p>To producers and development executives who work in these lanes: if you are developing one of the scripts on this list — or working on anything that lives in the territory we have described here — we would genuinely like to compare notes. Not to pitch you. Just to talk. We have found things we think are being missed, and we are curious whether your read matches ours. Reach out.</p>

<p>To writers: keep leaning into your vision. The next era of distribution rewards distinctive voices in ways that traditional theatrical never could. Whether or not you are on a list, whether or not you have relationships — reach out. The work has more paths than it has ever had.</p>
